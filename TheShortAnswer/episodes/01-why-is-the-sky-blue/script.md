# 01 — Why Is the Sky Blue?

- **Category:** Science & nature
- **Length:** ~29s (76 words)
- **Core idea:** Short (blue) wavelengths scatter off air molecules far more than long (red) ones.
- **Hook:** Earth's glowing atmosphere from orbit + "Why is the sky blue?"
- **Accent colour:** sky blue `(125, 200, 255)`
- **Production:** ✅ rendered (`out/` in this folder)

## Narration script

> Why is the sky blue?
> Sunlight looks white, but it's actually every color mixed together.
> In the atmosphere, it crashes into trillions of tiny air molecules.
> Blue light travels in short, choppy waves, so it gets scattered everywhere.
> Red light's long, lazy waves mostly pass straight through.
> So wherever you look, scattered blue light is coming at you.
> At sunset, light crosses so much more air that the blue scatters away, leaving the reds and oranges.

## Fact-check notes

- This is **Rayleigh scattering**: scattering strength ∝ 1/λ⁴, so 450 nm blue scatters about (700/450)⁴ ≈ 5.9× more than 700 nm red. That's the source of the "≈ 6×" label.
- Air at sea level has ~2.5 × 10²⁵ molecules per m³, about 78% N₂ and 21% O₂.
- Near the horizon, sunlight passes through roughly 38× the air mass it does at the zenith, hence "up to 40× more air".
- **"Why not violet?"**: the Sun emits less violet than blue, some is absorbed high in the atmosphere, and our eyes are less sensitive to it.
- The "waves crash into molecules" wording is a simplification: light's electric field makes molecules' electrons oscillate and re-radiate.
