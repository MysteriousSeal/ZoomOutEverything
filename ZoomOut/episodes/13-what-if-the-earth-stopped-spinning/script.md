> What if the Earth suddenly stopped spinning?
> At the equator, | you're moving at about one thousand six hundred seventy kilometers an hour.
> You don't feel it, because everything moves with you.
> Stop the planet suddenly, | and anything not bolted down keeps going east, at that speed.

> Over time, the oceans would start to move.
> Spinning makes Earth bulge about twenty-one kilometers at the equator.
> Without it, that water slides toward the poles.
> Two polar oceans, and one giant continent wrapped around the middle.
> And one day would last a whole year: | six months of daylight, six months of night.
> The strange part? | Earth really is slowing down.
> About four hundred million years ago, | a year had roughly four hundred days.

## Fact-check notes

- **1,670 km/h:** Earth's equatorial circumference 40,075 km ÷ one rotation (sidereal day, 23.934 h) = 1,674 km/h ≈ 1,670 km/h (1,040 mph). Using the 24 h solar day gives 1,670 km/h. Speed falls with latitude (× cos latitude).
- **Everything keeps going east at that speed:** inertia. Objects on the ground move with the surface; if the surface stopped instantly, unattached objects keep their ~1,670 km/h eastward velocity (at the equator; less at higher latitudes). The narration places it "at the equator" in line 1.
- **Winds of more than 1,000 km/h:** in the simplest model the atmosphere keeps its momentum relative to the stopped ground: ~1,670 km/h at the equator, still > 1,000 km/h out to ~53° latitude (1,670 × cos 53° ≈ 1,000). On screen: 620+ mph.
- **21 km bulge:** equatorial radius 6,378.1 km − polar radius 6,356.8 km = 21.4 km (WGS 84). The oceans follow this shape (the geoid). Without rotation the water's shape would relax toward a sphere while the rock bulge stays for a very long time, so the water drains off the "high" equator toward the poles. Model: Witold Fraczek, Esri (2010), "Why Bother to Stop the Earth's Rotation?", which shows two polar oceans and one continuous equatorial landmass. "Over time" is used because this is a model of the end state, not an instant event. On screen: ≈ 21 km / 13 mi, with the bulge drawn exaggerated and labelled as such.
- **One day = one year:** with no rotation relative to the stars, the Sun appears to move across the sky only once per orbit. A solar day lasts a year: roughly six months of daylight, six months of night at most places (the equator exactly; seasons complicate higher latitudes).
- **Earth is slowing down:** tidal friction with the Moon. Eclipse records show the day lengthening by ~1.8 ms per century on average (Stephenson, Morrison & Hohenkerk 2016, Proc. R. Soc. A); the tidal part alone is ~2.3 ms/century. On screen: "+ ~2 ms per century".
- **400 days, 400 million years ago:** John W. Wells (1963), "Coral growth and geochronometry", Nature 197: 948–950. Fine daily growth ridges on Middle Devonian corals (incl. *Heliophyllum* from New York) give about 400 per yearly band; Middle Devonian ≈ 393–383 million years ago, so "about 400 million years ago" and "roughly 400 days" are rounded honestly. Day length then ≈ 365.25 × 24 / 400 ≈ 22 h (the length of the year itself barely changes).
- **Coral location:** CORAL in `camera.js` sits on the Devonian rocks of upstate New York (Hamilton Group), a classic source of these fossils.
