# 11 — What If the Moon Disappeared? (60 s)

- **Series:** What If
- **Anchor:** a calm beach at night, under a full Moon
- **Ladder:** full-Moon nights ~100× darker (≈ 0.1–0.3 lux down to starlight ≈ 0.002 lux) → tides shrink to about a third of today's biggest (only the Sun's pull, ~46% of the Moon's, is left) → total solar eclipses gone forever → without the Moon steadying it, Earth's axial tilt could wobble far more over millions of years → seasons go haywire
- **Reveal:** the Moon is leaving anyway: about 3.8 cm a year, roughly as fast as your fingernails grow

## Narration script

> What if the Moon suddenly disappeared?
> The first thing you'd notice would be the nights.
> Today, a full Moon lights the land with reflected sunlight.
> Without it, | those nights would be about a hundred times darker.
> Then the tides. | The Moon does most of the pulling on our oceans.
> With only the Sun left, | tides would shrink to about a third of today's biggest.
> And total solar eclipses would be gone, forever.
> The Moon also steadies Earth's tilt, | the reason we have seasons.
> Without it, | many models say that tilt could wobble far more.
> Over millions of years, | seasons could go haywire.
> But here's the twist: | the Moon is already slowly leaving us.
> Every year, | it drifts about three point eight centimeters farther away.
> That's about as fast as your fingernails grow.

## Fact-check notes

- **Moonlight:** a full Moon gives about 0.05–0.3 lux at the ground (≈ 0.1 lux typical at mid-latitudes, up to ~0.3 lux with the Moon high in a clear sky). A clear moonless sky (starlight + airglow) gives about 0.001–0.002 lux. 0.1–0.3 / 0.002 ≈ 50–150×, so "about a hundred times darker" holds for nights that would have had a bright Moon. New-moon nights would not change: say "those nights" (the full-Moon nights), as the script does.
- **Moonlight is reflected sunlight:** the Moon's albedo is about 0.12.
- **Tides:** tidal force ∝ M / d³. Sun: 1.989e30 / (1.496e11)³. Moon: 7.342e22 / (3.844e8)³. Sun/Moon ≈ 0.46. Spring tides ∝ 1 + 0.46 = 1.46; Sun only ∝ 0.46, and 0.46 / 1.46 ≈ 0.32, so "about a third of today's biggest" (spring) tides. (Against an average tide it is closer to half.) This is the equilibrium-tide model: real coastal ranges are amplified by basin resonance, so read it as the simplest-model ratio. "The Moon does most of the pulling": the Moon's share is 1 / 1.46 ≈ 68%.
- **Eclipses:** total (and annular) solar eclipses need the Moon to cover the Sun. No other body can. Lunar eclipses would also go.
- **Tilt:** today's obliquity is 23.44°, varying between about 22.1° and 24.5° over a ~41,000-year cycle, damped by the Moon's torque on Earth's bulge. Without the Moon: Laskar, Joutel & Robutel (1993, *Nature* 361, 615) found chaotic swings from about 0° to 85°. Lissauer, Barnes & Chambers (2012, *Icarus* 217, 77) found smaller swings (mostly ±10–20°) over billions of years. Hence "many models say… could wobble far more" and "over millions of years". Don't put 0–85° on screen as fact.
- **Recession:** 3.82 ± 0.07 cm/yr from lunar laser ranging off the Apollo retroreflectors (Dickey et al. 1994, *Science* 265, 482). Narrated as "about three point eight centimeters". It is driven by tidal friction: Earth's spin slows and the Moon gains orbital energy.
- **Fingernails:** about 3.5 mm a month (Yaemsiri et al. 2010, *J. Eur. Acad. Dermatol. Venereol.*: 3.47 mm/month), so ≈ 4.2 cm a year; many sources give ~3–4 cm/yr. "About as fast" is fair.
- **Distances:** mean Earth–Moon centre distance 384,400 km (238,900 mi). Moon diameter 3,474 km. 3.8 cm = 1.5 in.
- **Ghost rings (beat 11):** at today's rate, 1 billion years × 3.8 cm = 38,000 km. Label any ring as "at today's rate" (the rate has varied).
- **Pinned comment:** 1969 → 2026 = 57 years × 3.8 cm ≈ 2.2 m ("about 2 metres").
