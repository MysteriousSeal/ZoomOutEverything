# Zoom Out: The Man Who Saved the World

**Series:** Hidden History · **Length:** ≈ 67.4 s · **Voice:** ElevenLabs Brittney (default) · **Hook:** shocking claim, readable on frame 1 · **Ending:** question for the comments

## Narration ("|" = phrase pause)

0. In 1983, one man stopped a nuclear war | by refusing to believe a computer.
1. September twenty-sixth. A secret bunker near Moscow. | Just after midnight. | Three weeks earlier, the Soviets had shot down a passenger jet. Tension is at its peak.
2. The alarm goes off: a US missile launch. | Then another. | Five in total. | The computer rates it: high reliability.
3. Stanislav Petrov has minutes to report it. | A report could trigger a counterstrike, | and a full nuclear war.
4. But a real attack would be hundreds of missiles, not five. | And the ground radar sees nothing.
5. He calls it a false alarm. | And waits. | Twenty-three minutes later, nothing has happened.
6. He was right. | Sunlight on high clouds had fooled the satellites. | The story stayed secret for fifteen years.
7. Would you have trusted the computer? | Tell me in the comments.

## FACT-CHECK (every number on screen must match this)

- **Date and time:** 26 September 1983, shortly after midnight Moscow time (the alarm is usually given as 00:14–00:15).
- **Place:** Serpukhov-15, the secret command centre of the Soviet early-warning satellite system (Oko), south of Moscow (≈ 70 km from the city centre). "Near Moscow" is accurate.
- **The officer:** Lieutenant Colonel Stanislav Yevgrafovich Petrov (1939–2017), duty officer that night, Soviet Air Defence Forces.
- **The alarm:** an Oko satellite (Kosmos 1382, in a highly elliptical Molniya orbit) reported one Minuteman ICBM launched from the United States, then four more: **5 in total**. The board showed "СТАРТ" (LAUNCH) with reliability rated "ВЫСОКАЯ" (HIGH).
- **Time pressure:** an ICBM flight from the US to the USSR takes roughly 20–30 minutes; the warning had to go up the chain within minutes. Soviet doctrine was launch-on-warning, so a confirmed report could lead to a retaliatory strike. The decision was the leadership's, not Petrov's: say "could trigger", never "he would have launched".
- **His reasoning:** a real US first strike would use hundreds of missiles, not five; the satellite system was new; ground-based radars showed no confirmation. He reported it to his superiors as a system malfunction (a false alarm).
- **The wait:** Petrov later said that after about 23 minutes with nothing happening, he knew he'd been right.
- **The cause:** sunlight reflecting off high-altitude clouds over the US, at an angle that lined up with the satellite's view (close to the autumn equinox), which the satellite read as rocket-engine flares.
- **Context:** three weeks earlier (1 September 1983) the USSR shot down Korean Air Lines Flight 007; tension was at a Cold War high.
- **Afterwards:** the incident stayed secret until the 1990s. Petrov received no reward at the time; he was later honoured internationally (e.g. the World Citizen Award 2006, the Dresden Peace Prize 2013).
- Avoid: "he pressed / didn't press the button" (there was no button), "he disobeyed orders", exact missile bases beyond "the United States".
- **Added lines (60 s version):** KAL 007 was shot down on 1 Sep 1983, 269 people killed ("three weeks earlier" ✓). The board rated the launch reliability "HIGH". Petrov said about 23 minutes passed before he was sure (00:15 → ≈ 00:38). The incident became public in 1998 (General Votintsev's memoirs): "secret for fifteen years" ✓.
