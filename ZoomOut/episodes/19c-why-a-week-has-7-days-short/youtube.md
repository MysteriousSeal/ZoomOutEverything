## Title
Nothing in the Sky Takes a Week. So Why 7 Days?

## Description
In 1793, France tried to replace the week with ten days. A day is one spin of the Earth, a month is about one lunar cycle, a year is one trip around the Sun. But nothing in the sky takes a week. So where does the seven come from? Many historians link the number to Babylon's seven moving lights: the Sun, the Moon and five planets. In Roman times, astrologers gave each day to one of them, which is why Saturday, Sunday and Monday still carry the names of Saturn, the Sun and the Moon. France and the Soviet Union both tried to replace it. The seven-day week outlived them all.

Under the Soviet five-day week, did everyone get the same day off? Yes, one shared day off, or no, it rotated? Tell me in the comments.

## Real answers (for reference, not for posting)
B, it rotated. The Soviet "nepreryvka" (continuous week), brought in from 1 October 1929, split each workforce into five groups, each marked by a colour (commonly yellow, pink/peach, red, purple, green) or a Roman numeral I-V, with staggered rest days. About 80% of workers were on the job on any given day and 20% were resting, so factories never stopped, and there was no common day off. Stalin condemned it in June 1931; most industry moved from late 1931 to a six-day week with a shared rest day (the 6th, 12th, 18th, 24th and 30th). The seven-day week returned by a decree of 26 June 1940. Sources: Zerubavel 1985, pp. 35-43; Foss 2004 (History Today); Frost 2018 (History.com); Schwarz 1931 (ILR); Wikipedia "Soviet calendar". Both Soviet calendar pages are drawn plain in this cut, so the answer never appears in the film.
