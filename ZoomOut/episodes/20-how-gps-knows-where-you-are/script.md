# 20 — How Does GPS Know Where You Are? (90 s)

- **Series:** How It Works
- **Anchor:** the blue dot on your phone's map
- **Ladder:** about 31 GPS satellites, ~20,200 km up → each laps Earth twice a day → atomic clocks on board → they broadcast position + exact time → your phone only listens → the signal takes ~0.07 s → light covers ~30 cm per nanosecond → 1 µs of error = 300 m → one satellite = a sphere, two = a circle, three = two points, four = one point + your phone's clock error
- **Reveal:** Einstein is in your pocket. Up there, clocks gain ~45 µs a day from weaker gravity and lose ~7 µs from their speed: net +38 µs a day. Uncorrected, GPS would drift about 10 km a day, so the clocks are tuned to tick slower before launch.

- **Accent colour:** signal green `(120, 230, 140)` · the location dot stays the iconic map blue `(70, 150, 255)`
- **Music:** `bright`

## Narration script

> How does your phone know exactly where you are?
> The answer is falling from space, | and the strangest part involves Einstein.
> About thirty-one GPS satellites orbit the Earth, | some twenty thousand kilometers up.
> Each one laps the planet about twice a day.
> They carry atomic clocks on board.
> They constantly broadcast where they are, | and the exact time.
> Your phone never transmits back. | It only listens.
> Those signals reach you in about seven hundredths of a second.
> But light covers about thirty centimeters in a billionth of a second.
> Off by a millionth of a second, | and you'd miss by three hundred meters.
> One satellite's delay puts you somewhere on a giant sphere.
> A second satellite, | and the two spheres meet in a circle.
> A third narrows it to two points, | one of them usually far out in space.
> A fourth fixes your phone's own clock, | which is nowhere near atomic.
> Now the strangest part: | Einstein is in your pocket.
> Up there, weaker gravity makes their clocks run faster, | by about forty-five microseconds a day.
> Their speed slows them by about seven. | So they gain thirty-eight every day.
> Left uncorrected, | GPS would drift about ten kilometers every day.
> So engineers tune the clocks to tick slower before launch, | and the dot stays on you.

## Fact-check notes

- **Satellites:** 31 operational GPS satellites (GPS.gov, Space Segment, 2024–2025). The baseline is 24 slots in 6 orbital planes, and the extras are "expandable" slots. Hence "about thirty-one". Check gps.gov before publishing, because the count varies by 1–2.
- **Altitude:** ~20,200 km (MEO, semi-major axis ≈ 26,560 km). 20,200 km ≈ 12,550 mi. Narrated as "some twenty thousand kilometers".
- **Period:** 11 h 58 min, which is half a sidereal day (23 h 56 min), so each satellite laps "about twice a day". The film's time-lapse runs one sidereal day: 2 laps, and Earth turns once. Inclination ~55°.
- **Atomic clocks:** each satellite carries rubidium and/or caesium atomic frequency standards (GPS.gov, USNO).
- **Broadcast:** the navigation message carries the satellite's ephemeris (precise orbit, i.e. position) and clock data with the time of transmission. Receivers are passive: GPS is one-way, and the phone never transmits to the satellites.
- **Signal travel time:** 20,200 km ÷ 299,792 km/s = 0.067 s straight overhead. A satellite near the horizon is ~25,800 km away, which takes ~0.086 s. "About seven hundredths of a second" = 0.07 s. The film's satellite B is 20,437 km away: 0.068 s.
- **30 cm per ns:** c × 1 ns = 0.2998 m ≈ 30 cm ≈ 1 ft.
- **1 µs → 300 m:** c × 1 µs = 299.8 m ≈ 984 ft ("about 1,000 ft"). This shows why timing must be good to nanoseconds.
- **Trilateration:**
  - Range from one satellite = a sphere. Two spheres intersect in a circle, and three spheres in (generally) two points. One of those is usually far off the Earth's surface or physically implausible, hence "usually".
  - The 4th measurement solves for the receiver's own clock bias, the fourth unknown (x, y, z, t). A phone has a quartz clock, not an atomic one.
  - The 2D picture-plane drawing is a schematic of this (the hero satellites are placed in one plane so the circles pass exactly through the phone).
- **Relativity:**
  - General relativity: the satellite clocks run fast by ≈ +45.7 µs/day because the gravitational potential is weaker at 20,200 km.
  - Special relativity: orbital speed ≈ 3.87 km/s, so the clocks run slow by ≈ −7.2 µs/day.
  - Net ≈ +38.5 µs/day.
  - Sources: N. Ashby, "Relativity in the Global Positioning System", Living Reviews in Relativity 6 (2003). Also Ohio State/Pogge, "Real-World Relativity: The GPS Navigation System".
- **10 km/day:** 38 µs × c ≈ 11.4 km of range error accumulated per day. This is the standard back-of-envelope figure ("about ten kilometers"). How a real receiver solution would degrade is more complicated (a shared clock offset is partly absorbed into the receiver's clock solution), which is why the narration says "drift" and "about".
- **Tuned before launch:**
  - The satellite clocks' 10.23 MHz fundamental frequency is offset to 10.22999999543 MHz (fractional −4.465 × 10⁻¹⁰), so it reads 10.23 MHz from the ground.
  - Receivers also apply a small periodic correction for orbital eccentricity.
- **Accuracy readout ±5 m:** GPS-enabled smartphones are typically accurate to within ~4.9 m under open sky (GPS.gov).
- **Location in the HUD:** 40.7580° N, 73.9855° W (Midtown Manhattan). A 10 km east drift = +0.1187° of longitude at that latitude.
