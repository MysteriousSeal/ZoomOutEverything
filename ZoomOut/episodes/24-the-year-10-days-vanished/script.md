# 24 — The Year 10 Days Vanished (hook format, ~77.9 s)

Series: **Hidden History**. Folder: `ZoomOut/episodes/24-the-year-10-days-vanished/`.

- **Anchor:** a printed October 1582 calendar page. Thursday 4 is followed by Friday 15.
- **Ladder:** the torn page → Caesar's calendar (46 BC) → a leap day every 4 years (365¼ d) → the real year returns to the equinox 11 min sooner → 1 day every 128 years → 10 days by 1582 (Easter drifting) → Gregory XIII deletes them → the 400-year rule (1900 no leap day, 2000 yes) → Britain skips 11 days in 1752 → today: 1 day per ~3,000 years.
- **Reveal:** the "missing" days are the sum of an 11-minute-a-year error, and the calendar still drifts today.
- **Length:** ~77.9 s including the preroll and the question (15 voice lines)
- **Accent colour:** parchment gold `(230, 190, 110)` · music `deep` · voice af_heart @ 0.92
- **Signature transition:** one true-scale orbit. From the full orbit the camera dives four orders of magnitude into the spring-equinox point, onto two real-coastline Earths 11 minutes of orbit apart (≈ 20,000 km). It then pulls back out continuously while the gap grows to 1 day and then 10 days, with a year counter racing from AD 325 to 1582. A second signature is the tear: days 5–14 lift out of the page and the rest of October slides back so that 15 lands on Friday.

## Narration script

> *(preroll, over the slowed tear)* Ten days in 1582 never happened. | Not one.
>
> How did ten days vanish from history?
> In 1582, much of Catholic Europe went to bed on Thursday, October fourth, | and woke up on Friday the fifteenth.
> The culprit: Julius Caesar's calendar, sixteen centuries old.
> A leap day every four years made its average year | three hundred sixty-five and a quarter days.
> But Earth returns to the equinox about eleven minutes sooner.
> Eleven minutes a year doesn't sound like much.
> But it adds up to a whole day every hundred and twenty-eight years.
> By 1582 it had slipped ten days, | and Easter was drifting away from the real equinox.
> So Pope Gregory the Thirteenth simply deleted them.
> To stop the drift, | three leap days every four hundred years were cancelled.
> That's why 1900 had no leap day, | but 2000 did.
> Britain held out until 1752, | and had to skip eleven days.
> Even today, the calendar drifts about a day every three thousand years.
> If ten days vanished from this year, | which ten would you skip? | Tell me in the comments.

TTS: years are written as digits and spoken via `{1582~fifteen eighty-two}`, `{1752~seventeen fifty-two}`, `{1900~nineteen hundred}` and `{2000~two thousand}`. STT check: "Gregory XIII", "Julius Caesar's" and "equinox" are all clean.

Wording changes from the draft, made for accuracy and length:
- "Earth laps the Sun 11 minutes faster" became "returns to the equinox 11 minutes sooner". The 365.2422-day year is the *tropical* year, measured equinox to equinox. One lap relative to the stars (the sidereal year, 365.2564 d) is actually longer than 365.25 d, so "laps the Sun" would be wrong.
- "every year was 365¼ days" became "its average year". Individual years have 365 or 366 days.
- "Easter drifting from the spring equinox" became "from the real equinox". The church's computus kept the equinox at March 21; the sky's equinox had moved to about March 11.
- Several lines were tightened to fit 68–78 s at speed 0.92.

## FACT-CHECK

- **The Julian calendar.** Julius Caesar introduced it in 46 BC (the "year of confusion", 445 days long). It took effect on 1 January 45 BC, with a leap day every 4th year, so its mean year is **365.25 d**. ✔ ("46 BC" on the coin tag is the reform year.) From 46/45 BC to AD 1582 is ≈ 1,626 years, so "sixteen centuries old" ✔.
- **The year of the seasons.** The mean tropical year is **365.24219 d**.
  - 365.25 − 365.24219 = **0.00781 d = 11 min 14.8 s** ✔ ("about eleven minutes").
  - 1 / 0.00781 = **128 years** per day ✔.
  - Note: the sidereal year (one lap relative to the stars) is 365.25636 d. That is why the line says Earth "returns to the equinox" sooner, not "laps the Sun" faster.
- **Scale of 11 minutes.** Earth's mean orbital speed is 29.78 km/s; × 674.8 s = **20,100 km** ≈ 1.58 Earth diameters (12,742 km). The film's orbit is true scale: R = 9,000 u = 1 AU, Earth radius 0.383 u, the 11-min gap 1.21 u. ✔
- **Why 10 days, not 13.**
  - The Council of Nicaea (AD 325) worked with the vernal equinox on March 21, and the 1582 reform restored *that* alignment, not the one of Caesar's time.
  - 1582 − 325 = 1,257 years; 1,257 / 128 = **9.8 ≈ 10 days** ✔.
  - The film's drift counter starts at AD 325 (tagged "counting from AD 325, Council of Nicaea"): 1 day at AD 453, 9.82 days at AD 1582.
- **Easter.**
  - The Easter computus fixed the vernal equinox at **March 21**. By 1582 the astronomical equinox fell around **March 11** (Julian).
  - So the date Easter was computed from had drifted ~10 days away from the real equinox, and Easter was moving later in the season. The line "Easter was drifting away from the real equinox" is accurate. ✔
- **Inter gravissimas.**
  - Gregory XIII's papal bull is dated **24 February 1582**.
  - Thursday 4 October 1582 was followed by **Friday 15 October 1582**. The weekday cycle was unbroken, which is why 15 sits in the Friday column. ✔
  - October 1582 began on a Monday ✔.
- **"Much of Catholic Europe."**
  - On 4/15 October 1582 the switch was made by Spain (and its realms), Portugal, the Papal States and most of Italy, and the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth.
  - France followed in December 1582 (9 → 20 Dec); the Catholic Low Countries and German states in 1582–84.
  - "Much of", not "all", is correct. ✔
- **The new leap rule.**
  - Century years are leap years only if divisible by 400. 1700, 1800 and 1900 were not; 1600 and 2000 were. That is 3 leap days dropped per 400 years ✔.
  - Mini-calendars: 1 Feb 1900 was a Thursday (28 days); 1 Feb 2000 was a Tuesday (29 days). ✔
- **Britain and its colonies.**
  - Under the Calendar (New Style) Act 1750, **Wednesday 2 September 1752 was followed by Thursday 14 September 1752**. 11 days were skipped, because the Julian calendar had kept 1700 as a leap year. ✔
  - 1 September 1752 was a Tuesday ✔.
- **Today.** The Gregorian mean year is **365.2425 d**. 365.2425 − 365.24219 = 0.00031 d ≈ **26.8 s per year**, so 1 day per ≈ **3,200 years** ("about three thousand"). Measured against the vernal-equinox year (365.2424 d) the drift is slower still. The film shows ≈ 1 day by ≈ AD 4,800.
- **Not used:** the "Give us our eleven days" riots. They are largely a myth: the slogan comes from Hogarth's 1755 election print, and there is no good evidence of actual riots.
- **Cards:** the coldest 10, the 10 before payday, I'd keep them all. These are opinion choices; no facts are involved.
